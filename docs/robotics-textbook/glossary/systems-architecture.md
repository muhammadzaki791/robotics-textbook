# Systems and Architecture

**Robotics Operating System (ROS)**: Flexible framework for writing robot software, providing services designed for a heterogeneous computer cluster such as hardware abstraction, device drivers, libraries, visualizers, message-passing, and package management.

**Distributed Intelligence**: Intelligence that emerges from the interaction of multiple simple agents, as opposed to centralized intelligence.

**Swarm Robotics**: The coordination of multiple robots working together to achieve collective behavior and accomplish tasks.

**Whole-Body Control**: A control approach that coordinates all of a robot's degrees of freedom simultaneously to achieve multiple objectives such as balance, manipulation, and obstacle avoidance.

**Sim-to-Real Transfer**: The process of transferring policies or models trained in simulation to real robotic systems, addressing the reality gap between simulation and reality.

**Long-Term Autonomy**: The ability of robots to operate independently for extended periods, handling various challenges and maintaining performance over time.

**General-Purpose Robot**: A robot capable of performing a wide variety of tasks rather than being specialized for specific functions.

**Safety-Critical System**: A system whose failure could result in loss of life, significant property damage, or environmental harm, requiring special attention to safety in design and operation.

**Inertial Measurement Unit (IMU)**: A device that measures and reports a body's specific force, angular rate, and sometimes the magnetic field surrounding the body, used for navigation and balance control.

**Optimization**: The process of making a system or design as effective or functional as possible, fundamental to many robotics problems including motion planning and control.

**Heuristic**: A practical approach to problem-solving that may not guarantee optimal results but is sufficient for reaching immediate goals, often used in robot planning.

**Telerobotics**: The remote control of robots, allowing human operators to control robots from a distance.

**Episodic Memory**: Memory of specific events and experiences, increasingly important in robots that need to remember and learn from specific interactions.

**Grounded Language Learning**: The process of learning language through physical interaction with the environment, important for robots that need to understand human language in context.

**Non-Holonomic Constraint**: A constraint on a robot's motion that cannot be integrated to form a constraint on position alone, common in wheeled robots and affecting motion planning.

**Utility Function**: A function that assigns a numerical value to different states or outcomes, representing the desirability of each state in decision-making processes.

**Artificial General Intelligence (AGI)**: Hypothetical AI that possesses the general intelligence of a human being, with the ability to understand or learn any intellectual task that a human being can.

**Actuator Saturation**: The condition where an actuator reaches its maximum or minimum output limit, which can affect control performance and stability.

**Adaptive Architecture**: A system architecture that can modify its structure or behavior in response to changing conditions or requirements.

**Anomaly Detection**: The process of identifying unusual patterns or behaviors that do not conform to expected norms, important for robot health monitoring.

**API (Application Programming Interface)**: A set of rules and protocols for building and interacting with software applications, crucial for robot software integration.

**Asynchronous Communication**: Communication between system components that does not require simultaneous participation, allowing for more flexible robot architectures.

**Autonomous Navigation**: The ability of a robot to move through an environment without human intervention, using sensors and planning algorithms.

**Bayesian Network**: A probabilistic graphical model that represents a set of variables and their conditional dependencies, used for robot decision making under uncertainty.

**Behavior Tree**: A hierarchical structure used to organize and execute robot behaviors, providing a flexible and modular approach to robot control.

**Bootstrapping**: The process of initializing a robot system from a minimal starting state, including sensor calibration and system diagnostics.

**Calibration**: The process of adjusting a sensor or system to ensure accurate measurements, critical for robot perception and control.

**Cloud Robotics**: The use of cloud computing to provide robots with additional computational power, data storage, and shared knowledge.

**Cognitive Architecture**: The structural organization of robot intelligence systems, defining how different cognitive components interact and process information.

**Command Interface**: The system through which humans or other systems communicate instructions to a robot, ranging from high-level task specifications to low-level motor commands.

**Computational Graph**: A directed graph where nodes represent mathematical operations and edges represent data flow, used in modern robot learning systems.

**Computational Load**: The amount of processing power required by robot algorithms, important for real-time performance and system design.

**Configuration Management**: The process of managing and tracking changes to robot software and hardware configurations over time.

**Context Awareness**: The ability of a robot system to understand and respond to environmental and situational context.

**Control Architecture**: The organizational structure of a robot's control system, including the hierarchy and interaction of different control layers.

**Cyber-Physical System**: A system that integrates computation, networking, and physical processes, which is fundamental to modern robotics.

**Data Pipeline**: The infrastructure for collecting, processing, and storing data in robot systems, crucial for learning and adaptation.

**Debugging**: The process of identifying and fixing errors in robot software, essential for reliable robot operation.

**Decentralized Control**: A control approach where decision-making is distributed among multiple agents or subsystems rather than centralized.

**Deep Learning Framework**: Software libraries that provide tools for implementing and training deep neural networks, essential for modern robot AI.

**Distributed Computing**: The use of multiple computing resources to solve problems that are too large for a single machine, important for robot learning and planning.

**Edge Computing**: Processing data near the source of generation rather than in a centralized data center, important for robot real-time performance.

**Embedded System**: A specialized computing system that is part of a larger device, typical of most robot hardware platforms.

**Event-Driven Architecture**: A software architecture where the flow of the program is determined by events such as sensor readings or user actions.

**Fault Tolerance**: The ability of a robot system to continue operating properly in the event of hardware or software failures.

**Feedback Loop**: A control system where the output is fed back as input to regulate the system's behavior, fundamental to robot control.

**Firmware**: Low-level software that provides control, monitoring, and data manipulation of electronic devices in robot systems.

**Fusion Architecture**: A system design that integrates information from multiple sensors or sources to provide more accurate and reliable robot perception.

**Hardware Abstraction Layer**: Software that allows higher-level programs to interact with hardware without needing to know the specific hardware details.

**Heterogeneous System**: A system composed of different types of components, such as various sensors, processors, and actuators in robots.

**Homogeneous System**: A system composed of similar or identical components, which can simplify design and maintenance.

**Hybrid Architecture**: A system that combines different approaches, such as symbolic and sub-symbolic AI, for improved robot performance.

**Interoperability**: The ability of different robot systems to work together and exchange information, important for multi-robot systems.

**Knowledge Representation**: The field of AI concerned with how information about the world can be represented in a form that a computer system can utilize.

**Latency**: The time delay between a command and its execution, critical for real-time robot performance.

**Learning Architecture**: The structural organization of machine learning components in a robot system, including training and inference pipelines.

**Localization**: The process of determining a robot's position and orientation in a known or unknown environment.

**Loop Rate**: The frequency at which a robot control loop executes, affecting the responsiveness and stability of robot behavior.

**Machine Learning Pipeline**: The complete process from data collection to model deployment for robot learning systems.

**Mapping**: The process of creating a representation of the environment, often combined with localization in SLAM systems.

**Middleware**: Software that provides common services and capabilities to applications beyond what's offered by the operating system.

**Modular Architecture**: A system design approach that separates functionality into independent, interchangeable modules, enabling flexible robot design.

**Multi-Agent System**: A system composed of multiple interacting intelligent agents, used in multi-robot coordination.

**Network Protocol**: A set of rules governing the exchange of information between robot systems, important for multi-robot communication.

**Onboard Computing**: The computational resources located on the robot itself, as opposed to remote or cloud computing.

**Open Source**: Software with source code that anyone can inspect, modify, and enhance, common in robotics development.

**Parallel Processing**: The execution of multiple computations simultaneously, important for real-time robot performance.

**Performance Metrics**: Quantitative measures used to evaluate the effectiveness and efficiency of robot systems.

**Pipeline Architecture**: A system design where data flows through a series of processing stages, common in robot perception systems.

**Real-Time System**: A system that must respond to inputs within strict time constraints, essential for many robot applications.

**Reconfigurable Architecture**: A system that can modify its structure or behavior to adapt to new tasks or environments.

**Resource Management**: The process of allocating and managing computational, memory, and energy resources in robot systems.

**Robot Middleware**: Software that facilitates communication and coordination between different robot software components.

**Safety Architecture**: The design of robot systems to ensure safe operation, including fail-safe mechanisms and safety protocols.

**Scalability**: The ability of a robot system to handle growth in complexity, size, or workload without degradation in performance.

**Scheduling**: The process of determining when and in what order robot tasks should be executed, important for real-time performance.

**Sensor Fusion**: The process of combining data from multiple sensors to achieve better performance than any individual sensor could provide.

**Simulation Environment**: A virtual environment used to model and test robot systems before deployment in the real world.

**Software Architecture**: The fundamental structures of a robot software system and the discipline of creating such structures.

**State Machine**: A model of computation used to design robot algorithms that can be in one of a finite number of states at any given time.

**System Integration**: The process of combining different robot subsystems into a functional whole, ensuring proper communication and coordination.

**Task Planning**: The process of determining a sequence of actions to achieve a goal, considering constraints and resources.

**Temporal Consistency**: The requirement that robot systems maintain proper timing relationships between different components and processes.

**Threading**: The use of multiple execution threads to improve robot system performance and responsiveness.

**Version Control**: Systems for managing changes to robot software over time, essential for collaborative development.

**Virtual Reality (VR)**: Computer-generated simulation of a three-dimensional environment that can be interacted with, used for robot programming and training.

**Visualization**: The process of creating visual representations of robot data, important for debugging and monitoring robot systems.

**Workflow**: The sequence of processes through which data flows in a robot system, from sensors to actuators.
